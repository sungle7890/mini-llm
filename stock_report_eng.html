Predicting Stock Direction —
And Why It Doesn't Work

A record of building a model from scratch to guess "will it go up or down tomorrow?" from past prices, then backtesting it with real dollars to test the question: "Can ML beat the market?"

Even built carefully (confidence thresholds, conservative trading), once you account for transaction cost and enlarge the sample, it can't beat simply buying and holding. That's not an opinion — it's the result of the experiment below.

Why It's Hard

Somewhere Between a Lottery and Text

Unlike a lottery, the market's signal is not zero. But the signal is weak, adversarial (it vanishes once discovered), and non-stationary (the rules keep changing) — and above all, it's easy to fool yourself. On text and addition (where there's real signal) learning worked, but on stocks it doesn't.

Reusable Pipeline

fetch → train → predict

  1. Fetch data — always up to the latest. fetch_data.py (no ticker = all, ticker given = that one) → {ticker}_data.json/.csv
  2. Train on everything → save — 15_stock_train.py → {ticker}_model.npz
  3. Predict — 16_stock_predict.py → next trading day P(up) + recommendation

Contents of the model file {ticker}_model.npz

Weights (W1·b1·W2·b2, input 15→hidden 64→2 classes) + standardization stats (mu·sd) + config/meta (K=15, ticker, last training date).

Trading rules (conservative confidence band)

P(up) ≥ 0.65  ->  buy (long)
P(up) ≤ 0.35  ->  cash (flat)
in between    ->  wait (hold previous position, no trade)

Experiment 1 · The Illusion

The Backtest Illusion — SPY, 15 Years

Chronological split (70% train / 30% future). On the training window alone it looks predictable, but on the future it collapses.

MetricValue
in-sample accuracy79% (illusion)
out-of-sample accuracy50.2% (coin flip)
buy & hold (future)1.82×
model strategy (cost 5bp)0.97× (loss)
strategy evaluated on the training window461.8× ← illusion!

The 461× is just memorizing the noise in the training data. On the future it collapses to 50%.

The FCEL Disaster — the trap of "sitting it out"

FCEL tripled under buy & hold (to $30,534), but the model, "not confident," sat in cash and ended at $8,530 (−$22,003). Market returns come from a handful of explosive stretches, and when a model that can't predict them is sitting out, it misses those jackpots.

The win rate swings from window to window (33% ↔ 43%) = luck, not skill. A real signal wouldn't wobble like that.

Lessons

What This Experiment Taught

  1. in-sample ≠ predictive power. The 79% training accuracy and 461× are just overfitting; the future is 50%.
  2. The more it overfits, the higher its confidence. QUBX, with little data, was 100% in-sample and 99.7% confident at prediction time — the signal it's most confident about is the one you can trust least.
  3. Transaction cost eats a thin edge. When direction is a coin flip, every buy and sell just leaks cost.
  4. The larger the sample, the worse it does. Going from 6 → 10 → 21 tickers, the overall loss becomes clearer.
  5. Short windows are dangerous. The "9 wins" of an 8-day backtest is luck. Judging anything takes a long window.
  6. The trap of "sitting it out." When a model that can't predict is on the sidelines, it misses the rare jackpots (FCEL, QUBX).
